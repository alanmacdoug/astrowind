# List Hygiene for Small Businesses: Why Dead Addresses Are Worse Than No List at All

For a long time, the logic of email marketing was simple: every address is an asset, a bigger list is a bigger asset, and you never voluntarily throw away assets.

That logic died when mailbox providers started scoring you on behaviour instead of volume. Today, an address that never opens, never clicks, and eventually bounces is not an asset. It is a liability wearing an asset's clothes.

This is the practical guide to keeping a list clean: how addresses decay, when to prune, how re-engagement works, and what a healthy list actually looks like.

## Why addresses go bad

Email addresses are perishable, and nobody notifies you when one expires:

- **People change jobs.** The corporate address your subscriber signed up with dies the day they do — and when it does, it hard bounces.
- **People abandon mailboxes.** The old Yahoo address from 2019, kept alive by your newsletter, eventually gets culled by the provider or fills up and soft-bounces forever.
- **People lose interest and say nothing.** No unsubscribe, no complaint — they just stop opening. The address still works, but it isn't listening anymore.
- **Some addresses were never real.** Typos at signup, throwaway addresses, bots filling your form. These were dead on arrival.

None of this means your list is bad. It means your list is alive, and living things accumulate waste.

## Why dead addresses are actively harmful

The passive reader costs you little on their own. The danger is what an email to a dead address *signals* to the systems deciding where your mail lands.

Sending to addresses that bounce is the clearest possible evidence of an unmaintained list. Sending in volume to addresses that never engage looks like spammer behaviour, because real businesses lose their readers gradually, not all at once. Mailbox providers don't evaluate your intentions — they evaluate your behaviour, and behaviour is downstream of list quality.

The brutal arithmetic: **a smaller, engaged list delivers better than a bigger, neglected one.** Five hundred people who open beat five thousand who don't — both in revenue, and in the sender reputation that decides where the next five hundred emails land.

## When to prune, and how

There is no universal calendar, but there are sensible rules of thumb:

1. **Immediately:** every hard bounce, every time. This should be automatic in your platform. Check that it is.
2. **After three to four consecutive sends with soft bounces:** treat the address as dead. Chronic "temporary" failure is permanent failure with better manners.
3. **After roughly a year of no engagement:** no opens, no clicks, nothing. This is the cohort that turns into bounces over the coming months — cut it before it converts itself into reputation damage.
4. **Before any send to a list that's been resting:** a list you haven't mailed in months will have decayed in the meantime. Prune before you send, never after.

If a year feels aggressive, that's what re-engagement campaigns are for — the intermediary step that gives dormant subscribers a graceful exit.

## How a re-engagement campaign works

Instead of deleting a year of silent subscribers outright, you send them one honest, well-crafted email:

- **Subject line states its purpose plainly.** "Are you still there?" or "Still want to hear from us?" — no trickery, no fake urgency.
- **The body makes leaving easy.** One clear choice: click to keep hearing from us, or unsubscribe. Some businesses offer an explicit downgrade option — "fewer emails" — which rescues a surprising number of the ambivalent.
- **The deadline is real.** Anyone who doesn't respond within a couple of weeks is unsubscribed. That's the point of the exercise: their silence is the answer.

The ones who click stay — refreshed, and now demonstrably engaged. The ones who don't were never coming back. You've traded phantom subscribers for a list that reflects reality, which is the only kind of list that performs.

## What to never do

- **Never buy or borrow a list.** Purchased addresses are unknown quantities with unknown histories, and the first send to them will bounce in volume. It's the fastest reputation burn there is, and the hardest to walk back.
- **Never resurrect an old list for one campaign.** "We still have the attendee list from 2023" — see above. A year of decay means a year of dead addresses, and cold infrastructure makes it worse.
- **Don't rely on unsubscribes to do the pruning.** Silent attrition is the norm; unsubscribing requires effort, and the disengaged don't make any.
- **Don't prune in anger after a bad result.** Hygiene is a routine, like servicing a vehicle. Work the calendar, not your frustration.

## What healthy looks like

A maintained small-business list isn't one that never decays — nothing achieves that. It's one where decay is **removed faster than it accumulates**: bounces suppressed on every send, dormant subscribers re-engaged or retired at least annually, and a list size you could defend line by line if asked "who is on here and why?"

Ask that question about your own list today. If there's a chunk of it you can't answer for, you've found your first pruning block.
